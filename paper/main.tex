\title{PIKG-RAG: Augmenting Hybrid Retrieval with\\
Hierarchical and Graph Structure for Thai Criminal\\
Law Question Answering}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Authors}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A retrieval-augmented system for Thai criminal-law question answering may retrieve the offence
section matching a query while omitting an unnamed general rule that changes the legal conclusion.
We study, on a 255-question benchmark of six task types drawn from the 444-section Thai Criminal
Code, whether the statute's authored hierarchy and a provision graph should \emph{replace} a strong
lexical--dense retriever or \emph{augment} it. The decisive result is directional and large:
navigating the hierarchy in place of retrieval (PageIndex) collapses answer accuracy to 0.414
against 0.736 for the hybrid baseline, and the ``full'' system built on that replacement ranks last
of every configuration. Recast additively as Page-Indexed Knowledge-Graph Augmented Generation (PIKG-RAG)---PageIndex
candidates unioned into the hybrid pool, and a graph-selected supporting section placed in a reserved
rank slot so it never displaces a primary section---the same components recover to 0.79--0.81,
matching or modestly exceeding the baseline while never underperforming it. We report this advantage cautiously: it is small (paired
$\Delta\approx+0.06$, range $+0.05$ to $+0.08$ across three generation runs), and it does not persist
under lay-language paraphrases of the questions ($n=40$), indicating that part of the benchmark's
retrievability rests on lexical overlap with the statute. Beyond the system, our analysis yields
transferable findings: recall does not predict accuracy (a fusion-tuned baseline reaches the highest
Recall@5, 0.959, yet lower accuracy); 719 text-derived \texttt{CITES} edges outperform 13{,}296
rule-materialized \texttt{APPLIES\_TO} edges, so edge precision matters more than count; and
generation variance is large enough at this scale to swamp fine-grained effects, which we therefore
report as exploratory. We release the pipeline, the benchmark, per-item rankings and judge verdicts,
and a locked run snapshot.
\end{abstract}

retrieval-augmented generation, knowledge graph, legal information retrieval, Thai NLP,

\section{Introduction}

Thai criminal-law question answering is structurally demanding because an offence may be modified
by a general rule elsewhere in the Code. Consider a 17-year-old who commits theft: \TH{มาตรา} 334
defines the offence and adult penalty, but the answer must also consider the juvenile rule in
\TH{มาตรา} 75, which can reduce the applicable punishment scale. The provisions share little
wording, so a similarity retriever may return \TH{มาตรา} 334 and miss \TH{มาตรา} 75. The resulting
answer can cite a genuinely relevant section yet still report the wrong legal consequence.

The Thai Criminal Code is a useful testbed for this failure. Its Book--Title--Chapter--Section
hierarchy is explicit, Book~1 rules repeatedly modify Book~2 offences~\cite{pythainlpthailaw}, and numbered penalties and
inserted provisions make omissions auditable. Modern legal RAG combines BM25
~\cite{robertson2009bm25}, dense retrieval~\cite{karpukhin2020dpr}, and retrieved evidence for
generation~\cite{lewis2020rag}. NitiBench further shows that section-level units outperform
fixed-length chunks in Thai law~\cite{akarajaradwong2025nitibench}. This motivates looking beyond
segmentation to the recovery of dependencies between complete sections.
Prior work has not directly established whether statutory structure should replace a strong
lexical--dense retriever or augment it, nor how supporting sections can enter a fixed context without
displacing primary sections.

PageIndex navigates an authored document hierarchy without embeddings~\cite{pageindex}. We initially
hypothesized that this navigation could replace conventional retrieval. We tested that design, but
standalone PageIndex reached only
0.414 answer accuracy against 0.736 for Hybrid. We then evaluated an additive design: PageIndex
candidates are unioned with the Hybrid pool, while a section-level knowledge
graph~\cite{edge2024graphrag,pan2024unifying} supplies one supporting section through a reserved
slot. This design is Page-Indexed Knowledge-Graph Augmented Generation (PIKG-RAG).

On the 220 answerable items of the benchmark, the additive system recovers to 0.79--0.81 and never
underperforms the hybrid baseline, whereas the replacement design collapses to 0.414. The central,
robust result is a design principle: for statutory QA, structure should augment a strong retriever,
not replace it. The additive system's advantage \emph{over} the baseline is real but small and
setting-dependent, and we treat it as such.

This work makes four contributions. First, a design principle established by a direct
replacement-vs-augmentation comparison under paired evaluation: replacing lexical--dense retrieval
with hierarchy navigation is catastrophic (0.414 vs 0.736), while augmenting it is safe. Second, a
mechanism---a reserved rank slot---that admits a graph-selected supporting section without displacing
primary evidence, and that we show never reduces accuracy. Third, an analysis with transferable
lessons for legal RAG: recall does not predict answer accuracy, edge precision outweighs edge count
(\texttt{CITES} $\gg$ \texttt{APPLIES\_TO}), and generation variance is large enough to require
multi-run reporting at this scale. Fourth, a released 255-item Thai statutory QA benchmark built by a
gold-by-construction protocol, together with an honest account of its lexical-overlap bias measured
via lay-language paraphrases.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Retrieval Foundations}

BM25 captures exact terminology~\cite{robertson2009bm25}, dense retrieval captures semantic
similarity~\cite{karpukhin2020dpr}, and cross-encoders rerank their candidate pools
~\cite{nogueira2019bertrank}. RAG supplies retrieved evidence to the generator
~\cite{lewis2020rag}, with later systems making retrieval adaptive~\cite{asai2024selfrag}. For Thai
law, section-level units outperform fixed-length chunks~\cite{akarajaradwong2025nitibench}; our
baseline therefore retrieves complete sections, leaving inter-section dependencies as the main
structural issue.

\subsection{Trees and Graphs}

RAPTOR induces a hierarchy by clustering and summarizing text~\cite{sarthi2024raptor}, whereas
PageIndex navigates an authored hierarchy without embeddings~\cite{pageindex}. This supports
vertical Book--Title--Chapter--Section search but not lateral statutory links. GraphRAG and related
work use graph structure to connect evidence~\cite{edge2024graphrag,pan2024unifying}; however, the
combination of authored-tree navigation and section-level graph traversal remains less studied.
Evidence position also affects model use~\cite{liu2024lostmiddle}, motivating controlled context
allocation.

\subsection{Legal QA}

Domain-specific models support legal NLP~\cite{chalkidis2020legalbert}, while legal RAG has combined
vector and graph retrieval~\cite{barron2026bridging}. NitiBench's NitiLink condition improves
cross-reference coverage but reports inconsistent generation gains
~\cite{akarajaradwong2025nitibench}. Our position is narrower: starting from a section-level
lexical--dense baseline, we test whether an authored hierarchy and provision graph should replace or
augment that baseline, and whether graph evidence can be admitted without displacing primary
sections. Unlike bridge-entity benchmarks~\cite{yang2018hotpotqa,trivedi2022musique}, statutory
multi-hop questions link an offence to a low-overlap applicable rule. We therefore measure primary
and supporting retrieval separately and validate the LLM judge~\cite{zheng2023judging} against human
ratings.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Corpus and Artifacts}

Our corpus is the 444-section Thai Criminal Code from the PyThaiNLP \texttt{criminal-csv-v0.1}
release~\cite{pythainlpthailaw}, which we treat as a locked snapshot: it fixes the text used in every
condition but documents no amendment date, so all penalty results apply to this snapshot rather than
to a named revision. Ingestion yields three artifacts. \texttt{sections.jsonl} holds one record per
\TH{มาตรา}, and every retriever uses this unit, so chunk size is constant across conditions.
\texttt{penal\_tree.json} encodes the authored hierarchy from Book (\TH{ภาค}) through Title
(\TH{ลักษณะ}) and Chapter (\TH{หมวด}) to Section (\TH{มาตรา}), the space PageIndex searches. A
knowledge graph holds 719 \texttt{CITES} and 13,296 \texttt{APPLIES\_TO} edges.

The two edge types differ in provenance. \texttt{CITES} comes from the statute itself, where one
section names another, so its precision follows from the text. \texttt{APPLIES\_TO} is
materialised from three scope rules---\texttt{ALL\_OFFENCES}, \texttt{book2}, and
\texttt{by\_penalty}---that link a Book-1 general provision to the offences it governs; none of
its 13,296 edges has been hand-verified.

\subsection{Evaluation Set Construction}
\label{sec:evalset}

Our 255-item benchmark follows a human-first protocol: the gold sections $g$, and the supporting
sections $u$ for multi-hop items, are designated manually before any model sees the item, so the
retrieval labels cannot be a model's invention. Only the text of those sections is then supplied to a
drafting model, which writes the question, a reference answer, and atomic validation facts using
nothing else; a validator rejects any item citing a non-existent provision. The drafter is neither
the generator under test nor the judge, so no component evaluates its own output. Items are sampled
across all \TH{ลักษณะ} by fixed-seed stratified sampling and cover six task types: lookup (75),
penalty (55), multi-hop (45), unanswerable (35), exception (25), and definition (20). Multi-hop
items pair a Book-2 offence with a Book-1 general provision---attempt (\TH{มาตรา} 80),
co-principals (83), accessories (86), and others---and a correct answer must cite both.

\subsection{Retrievers}

Given a query $q$, every component returns an ordered list of complete \TH{มาตรา} rather than text
fragments. Three components each act on a different signal.

The \textit{Hybrid} retriever fuses min--max-normalised BM25 and BGE-M3
scores~\cite{chen2024bgem3} with equal weights ($\alpha = 0.5$). Its top $M = 50$ sections form
$H(q)$ and are reranked by Qwen3-Reranker-4B~\cite{zhang2025qwen3embedding}.
\textit{PageIndex} has the generator descend \texttt{penal\_tree.json} with beam width $b = 5$,
choosing at each level which child headings to expand and returning the leaves it reaches,
$P\!I(q)$; no embeddings are used at any stage. \textit{Graph traversal} treats edges as undirected
and untyped, expanding a seed set by one hop to every section it \texttt{CITES} and every offence an
\texttt{APPLIES\_TO} rule governs. This route can retrieve provisions such as the juvenile
mitigation in \TH{มาตรา} 75 even when they share little wording with $q$.

\subsection{Additive Composition and the Reserved Slot}

The pipeline collects two kinds of evidence: primary sections and supporting sections.
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} summarises the composition. The primary pool is the union
$P(q) = H(q) \cup P\!I(q)$, reranked by the cross-encoder $\rho$.

Graph candidates cannot be merged into the same ranking without changing what the score means. A
provision reached through \texttt{APPLIES\_TO} may be legally necessary despite little lexical
overlap, so $\rho$ tends to rank it below primary offences; increasing its graph weight can instead
evict those offences. The reserved slot separates these objectives: primary relevance determines
the first $k-1=4$ positions, while graph connectivity determines rank $k$ ($k=5$), occupied by the
best graph-only provision $s^*$:
\begin{equation}
R(q)=
\begin{cases}
(\pi_1,\dots,\pi_{k-1},\,s^*) & \text{if }s^*\text{ exists},\\[2pt]
(\pi_1,\dots,\pi_k) & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:reserve}
\end{equation}
Thus the graph is guaranteed one opportunity to supply a missing rule, but it cannot consume any of
the four positions allocated to primary sections.

\subsection{Generation, Judging, and Statistical Metrics}

All final responses are generated using the open-weight model
Qwen3.6-35B-A3B~\cite{qwen36model} at temperature $0$, processing the top $k=5$ retrieved sections
without any Thai-specific fine-tuning. Every experimental condition shares an identical system
prompt to prevent task-type leakage. Furthermore, this same model powers PageIndex tree navigation,
holding the generator fixed across structural comparisons. Answer evaluation is conducted by
Gemma-3-27b~\cite{gemma3report}, from a different provider than the generator and dataset drafter,
which assigns an overall score from 1 to 5 and verifies each atomic claim against the retrieved
context. Answer accuracy is the fraction scoring 4 or 5; mean judge score is also reported.

Retrieval is scored with standard recall@5 and MRR@10 plus two multi-hop measures. Writing $R_k$
for the top-$k$ of the returned list,
\begin{align}
\mathrm{Cov}@k &= \mathbf{1}\big[(g\cup u)\subseteq R_k\big], &
\mathrm{Sup}@k &= \frac{|u\cap R_k|}{|u|}.
\end{align}
These measures capture supporting-section recovery that primary recall@5 omits. On the 220
answerable items, generation is scored by
answer accuracy against the reference, per-claim grounding (the fraction of atomic claims entailed by
the context), and the unretrieved-citation rate (the fraction of cited sections absent from the
context, a deterministic hallucination check). All conditions are evaluated on a paired per-item
basis.
We report 95\% confidence intervals derived from paired bootstrap resampling over $10{,}000$
iterations, evaluate statistical contrasts using McNemar's exact test on discordant
pairs~\cite{mcnemar1947}, and strictly control the family-wise error rate using the
Holm--Bonferroni method~\cite{holm1979simple} at the $\alpha = 0.05$ significance level. The
benchmark is split by type into 80 development items (69 answerable, 11 unanswerable) and 175
held-out test items (151 answerable, 24 unanswerable). Design and tuning decisions, including the
ablations of Section~\ref{sec:ablations}, use the development split; reported metrics use its
answerable subset, and the headline contrast is confirmed on the 151 answerable test items in
Section~\ref{sec:controls}.

\subsection{Main Retrieval and Accuracy Results}

\textbf{Replacement fails.} Standalone PageIndex performs far below Hybrid in recall (0.518 versus
0.927) and accuracy (0.414 versus 0.736), and the replacement full system ranks last of every
configuration. This gap is large and unambiguous, and is the paper's most robust finding: navigating
the authored hierarchy in place of a lexical--dense retriever is strictly worse on every measure.

\textbf{Augmentation is safe.} Unioning PageIndex candidates into the hybrid pool gives the highest
Recall@5, 0.950, and 0.786 accuracy; reserving the final slot for a graph provision lowers primary
recall slightly to 0.932 but raises support-recall from 0.378 to 0.556, coverage to 0.850, and
accuracy to 0.814. The additive components never underperform the baseline.

\textbf{Similarity misses supporting provisions.} The support-recall column isolates why structure is
needed: the dense retriever recovers almost none of the general provisions a multi-hop answer
requires (support-recall 0.022) and even BM25 reaches only 0.178, because these provisions share
little wording with the question. This is the empirical basis for adding a graph route.

Among the paired contrasts, the cumulative addition of both structural layers is the only one that
crosses the significance threshold after Holm--Bonferroni ($\Delta=+0.077$, $p=0.006$); each single
layer alone does not ($p=0.09$). We read this as an \emph{additive} effect rather than a synergy: a
bootstrap difference-in-differences finds no interaction between the two factors
($-0.005$, 95\% CI $[-0.073,+0.064]$), so the two increments simply sum to clear the threshold that
either alone falls short of at this sample size. We do not claim PIKG-RAG dominates HYBRID+PI, which
it does not distinguish from at $n=220$.

This advantage is also modest against generation noise. Re-running the generator three times (all at
temperature 0; MoE inference through one provider is not bit-deterministic), PIKG-RAG accuracy spans
0.777--0.814 (spread 0.036, roughly eight items) while Hybrid is more stable (0.727--0.736, spread
0.009); the reported 0.814 is the top of this range. The paired $\Delta$(FULL$-$HYB) is
$+0.077$, $+0.055$, $+0.050$ across the three runs---positive every time but averaging $\approx+0.06$
rather than a fixed $+0.077$. We therefore treat the headline advantage as a small, directionally
consistent effect, and report finer-grained per-type differences as exploratory (Section~V), since
they are smaller than this variance.

\subsection{The Graph Contribution Is Within Generation Variance}

We attempted to isolate the graph's own effect from the reserved slot's context change, and the
honest conclusion is that it cannot be cleanly separated at this scale. A clean control keeps all
five primary sections and adds the graph provision as a sixth (k$=$6): accuracy moves from 0.786
(HYBRID+PI, five primaries) to 0.795, a change of $+0.009$ that is well inside the $0.036$
generation-variance band measured above. The reserved-slot configuration (k$=$5, replacing the fifth
primary with the graph provision) reaches 0.814, but the differences among 0.786, 0.795, and 0.814
are all smaller than run-to-run variance. We therefore do not attach a precise number to the graph's
marginal contribution; what is stable is only that adding it does not hurt.

\subsection{Controls and Robustness}
\label{sec:controls}

\textbf{Tuned baseline.} Because PIKG-RAG has more moving parts than Hybrid, we also tune the
baseline's fusion weight $\alpha\in\{0.3,0.5,0.7\}$; it is best at $0.7$ (Recall@5 0.959, accuracy
0.759). Each system compared at its own best $\alpha$ (PIKG-RAG is best at $0.5$; $\alpha=0.7$
actually lowers it), PIKG-RAG leads by $+0.055$ (95\% CI $[+0.009,+0.100]$, $p=0.036$). Forcing both
to $\alpha=0.7$---which handicaps PIKG-RAG---shrinks the difference to $+0.023$ ($p=0.50$), consistent
with the small, variance-bounded advantage above. Notably the tuned baseline retrieves \emph{better}
(Recall@5 0.959 vs 0.932) yet answers no better, reinforcing that recall does not predict accuracy.

\textbf{Targeted vs.\ blind provisions.} Since every multi-hop supporting section is one of six Book-1
provisions, a trivial alternative is to append all six to every context. This reaches 0.622 multi-hop
and 0.805 overall---below graph selection (0.644, 0.814) while enlarging the context from five to ten
sections---so selecting one relevant provision is at least as good as, and far more efficient than,
including all six.

\textbf{Edge source.} Restricting the graph to the 719 text-derived \texttt{CITES} edges gives 0.791
overall / 0.511 multi-hop; restricting to the 13{,}296 rule-materialized \texttt{APPLIES\_TO} edges
gives only 0.768 / 0.467, despite $18\times$ more edges. Using both is best (0.814 / 0.644). Edge
\emph{precision} therefore matters more than count, and the two edge types are complementary---a
direct argument for curating \texttt{APPLIES\_TO} rather than expanding it.

\textbf{Refusal.} On the 35 out-of-scope items, PIKG-RAG correctly declines 33 while wrongly refusing
only 7 of 220 answerable items (refusal accuracy 0.965, highest of all arms; Hybrid 0.957). Refusal
is thus a strength rather than a gap; the earlier difficulty was a cue-list artifact in the
deterministic metric, which a phrase-robust check resolves.

\textbf{Held-out test.} Fixing every design and tuning decision on the 80-item development split, the
additive advantage transfers to the 151 answerable test items ($\Delta=+0.073$, 95\% CI
$[+0.007,+0.139]$, $p=0.043$).

\subsection{External Validity: Lay-Language Paraphrases}
\label{sec:paraphrase}

Because each question is drafted from its gold section's text, questions may share vocabulary with
the answer and favour lexical retrieval. To probe this, we rewrote 40 answerable items (stratified by
type) into everyday language that avoids statutory terms, reducing 3-gram overlap with the gold text
from 0.30 to 0.21. On these items, recall@5 falls for both systems (Hybrid $0.875\!\to\!0.800$,
PIKG-RAG $0.900\!\to\!0.800$) and the accuracy gap closes: PIKG-RAG$-$Hybrid moves from $+0.075$ on
the original wording to $+0.000$ on the paraphrases ($p=1.0$, $n=40$). We read this as two things at
once: part of the benchmark's retrievability rests on lexical overlap, and the additive advantage is
tied to that setting. The sample is small and underpowered, so this bounds rather than refutes the
effect; a larger paraphrase study is future work. It does, however, temper any claim that the
structural advantage transfers unchanged to naturally phrased user questions.

\section{Ablation Analysis and Diagnostics}
\label{sec:ablations}

Increasing the candidate pool from 50 to 100 raises Recall@5 from 0.932 to 0.945 but lowers overall
accuracy; multi-hop accuracy falls by 0.177. Under \texttt{ALL\_OFFENCES}, one expanded seed can
expose roughly 271
neighbors; this is conditional fan-out, not average graph degree. Additional seeds therefore add
substantial graph noise.

\subsection{The Recall--Accuracy Trade-Off}

PIKG-RAG uses one position for support, giving lower primary Recall@5 than HYBRID+PI (0.932 versus
0.950) but higher support-recall and accuracy (0.556 and 0.814). Adaptive gating raises primary
recall to 0.936 but lowers accuracy to 0.741, while the deeper pool reaches 0.945 recall at lower
overall and multi-hop accuracy. The tested variants therefore trade primary recall against supporting
evidence.

\subsection{Multi-Hop Failure Breakdown}

Multi-hop accuracy is 0.644 versus 0.844 for ORACLE. Retrieval misses account for 22\% of items and
generation failures for 13\%. Every missing support is graph-connected but outranked; 51\% involve
generic \texttt{ALL\_OFFENCES} edges. The failure pattern motivates more selective graph edges.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

PageIndex is ineffective as a Hybrid replacement, while the combined structural condition is the
only one that differs significantly from Hybrid. This is consistent with hierarchy and graph links
providing complementary signals. The graph effect is not uniform: the exploratory breakdown is
positive for multi-hop questions but slightly negative for several single-section types, so a static
reserved slot redistributes accuracy across tasks.

Primary recall is already high (0.932, or 0.945 with a deeper pool), whereas remaining errors cluster
in multi-hop retrieval and evidence use. Graph expansion exposes many weakly discriminative
neighbors, and none of the 13{,}296 \texttt{APPLIES\_TO} edges has been manually verified. These
observations motivate edge curation and conditional graph use rather than simply increasing search
depth.

The findings have several limits. Judge agreement is substantial overall ($\kappa=0.67$) but poor on
multi-hop items ($\kappa=0.13$), and only one human reviewer was available. Reference answers and
graph edges lack legal review. PageIndex also adds LLM calls absent from Hybrid, confounding hierarchy
with extra inference, and transfer to a Thai-adapted generator is untested. Finally, PIKG-RAG is
distinguishable from Hybrid but not HYBRID+PI at the reported sample size. The 35 unanswerable items
are not included in these metrics because a reliable refusal measure remains unavailable.

\section{Future Work}

The error breakdown motivates further curation of the \texttt{APPLIES\_TO} edges. The
\texttt{ALL\_OFFENCES} scope accounts for 67\% of all
edges and can expose many offense neighbors per expanded general-provision seed, making it weakly
discriminative. A
lawyer-reviewed pass that removes overly broad scopes and validates the specific \texttt{book2} and
\texttt{by\_penalty} edges could improve reserved-slot selection. Such a review could focus on an
estimated 40--60 general provisions rather than all 13,296 edges.

Improved edges may also support more effective dynamic gating. The current gate does not reliably
distinguish a relevant low-scoring provision from a generic neighbor, which may partly reflect edge
quality.

Three smaller steps follow. First, an LLM-selection condition over a flat candidate list, matched on
the number of reasoning calls, would separate gains from hierarchy and extra inference. Second, a
second legal reviewer would provide human--human agreement alongside the human--judge comparison.
Third, repairing the refusal metric would enable a dedicated analysis of the 35 unanswerable items.

Finally, the $2\times2$ GLMM interaction remains to be fitted; the current analysis
uses a bootstrap difference-in-differences in its place.

\section{Ethical Considerations}

The system retrieves and cites statutory provisions; it does not provide legal advice. Its outputs
require review by a qualified practitioner before they are relied upon. The principal deployment
risk is that a fluent answer with a plausible citation may still be legally wrong. This risk
motivates reporting a closed-book floor, an oracle ceiling, and citation-grounding measures alongside
the headline results.

The corpus consists of public law, is redistributed under its original license, and contains no
personal data.

\section{Conclusion}

Structure improves statutory retrieval only when added to a strong retriever rather than used in
its place. Replacing Hybrid retrieval with PageIndex tree navigation is worse on every tracked
measure, reaching 0.414 answer accuracy against 0.736. Layering the same structural components onto
Hybrid produces PIKG-RAG, which confines the knowledge graph to one reserved rank slot, reaches
0.814, and is the only
configuration that significantly beats the baseline after Holm--Bonferroni correction. Neither
structural component is significant alone, so the result concerns their composition rather than
either mechanism independently.

The controlled comparison and exploratory subgroup analysis suggest that the graph contribution is
concentrated on multi-hop questions rather than uniform across types. The observed retrieval errors
motivate further graph curation and evaluation of dynamic gating. We release the pipeline, the
255-item benchmark, and a locked run snapshot.
